# Aesthetic judgment

A design is not good because it matches a fashionable style. Judge whether its visual decisions form a coherent answer to the product, content, audience, medium, and desired emotional character.

## Durable criteria

Use these as questions, not numeric scores.

- **Fit** — does the visual language suit what the product is, who uses it, and what they need to feel or do?
- **Hierarchy** — can the eye tell what matters first, what supports it, and what is optional without decoration doing all the work?
- **Composition** — do scale, alignment, cropping, negative space, and visual mass create a deliberate whole rather than a stack of components?
- **Rhythm** — do repetition and variation create pace? Repeating everything is monotonous; varying everything destroys coherence.
- **Proportion and optical balance** — do elements feel balanced to the eye, including type, icons, borders, radii, image crops, and asymmetric layouts, rather than merely sharing mathematical values?
- **Typography** — do face, width, weight, case, line length, spacing, and role hierarchy reinforce the character and reading task?
- **Color, light, and material** — do palette, contrast, surface treatment, shadow, texture, and transparency describe one material world and preserve semantic meaning?
- **Specificity** — could the same design be relabeled for an unrelated company with almost no visual change? If yes, the direction is probably generic.
- **Tension and restraint** — is there enough contrast or surprise to create identity, but enough quiet structure that the memorable move has somewhere to land?
- **Finish** — do states, copy, icon weight, alignment, transitions, real content, and edge cases carry the same level of care as the hero view?

Beauty often comes from relationships rather than ingredients. A common font can look excellent with the right measure and hierarchy; an expensive font can look amateur when spacing, scale, or content is wrong.

## Read style by its own logic

No style below is a recommendation. When the brief chooses one, judge it by what makes that language work rather than forcing another style's virtues onto it.

| Direction | What usually makes it convincing | Common failure |
|---|---|---|
| Minimal / quiet | exact hierarchy, proportion, spacing, typography, material restraint | empty rather than calm; weak hierarchy hidden behind whitespace |
| Editorial | typographic rhythm, strong grid logic, meaningful asymmetry, image/caption relationships | costume-newspaper details with no editorial structure |
| Luxury / premium | restraint, material confidence, excellent type and imagery, controlled contrast | gold + serif + huge whitespace used as a shortcut for value |
| Technical / developer | precision, dense but legible information, useful states, disciplined accents | decorative terminal labels and fake metrics that communicate nothing |
| Data-heavy / dashboard | comparison, alignment, scanability, semantic color, density matched to task | every datum boxed into equal cards; decoration competing with data |
| Playful | controlled surprise, expressive shape/type/color, clear interaction feedback | random novelty, childishness, or too many competing gestures |
| Brutalist / raw | deliberate grid tension, direct typography, strong contrast, visible structure | accidental ugliness, poor reading order, inconsistency excused as rawness |
| Experimental / expressive | a clear concept carried through composition and interaction | novelty without subject relevance; wayfinding sacrificed for spectacle |
| Public / regulated / trust-first | clarity, predictability, accessibility, calm hierarchy, honest content | sterile genericism or decorative prestige signals that undermine trust |
| Consumer / lifestyle | approachable rhythm, strong imagery or product material, human copy | generic rounded friendliness with no product identity |

A style can intentionally violate a conventional rule if the violation strengthens its concept without breaking the task contract. The burden is to explain the visual role of the violation, not to conform to a universal taste checklist.

## Relational checks

Use rendered output, not token values alone.

- **Squint check:** blur or mentally squint at the page. The main hierarchy should still read through large masses and contrast.
- **Silhouette check:** ignore colors and copy. The composition should have a recognizable structure rather than a repeated stack of interchangeable blocks.
- **Grayscale check:** important hierarchy and state differences should not disappear when hue is removed unless color is intrinsically the content.
- **Content-swap check:** replace brand nouns and hero copy with a different industry. If the design still fits perfectly, find what can be derived more specifically from the real subject.
- **Decoration-removal check:** remove each decorative device mentally. If meaning, character, interaction, and composition do not suffer, the device must justify its maintenance cost another way or be removed.
- **Real-content check:** stress long labels, empty states, ugly data, multiple languages, and actual imagery. A direction that works only with ideal placeholder copy is not finished.
- **Signature check:** identify what the work will be remembered by. It may be typography, composition, material, imagery, interaction, or extreme restraint; it does not need to be an extra decorative object.

## Cross-brief calibration

When improving this skill itself, evaluate it on briefs that intentionally demand different visual languages. Compare the outputs at the system level: composition, type roles, density, material, imagery, and motion vocabulary.

If unrelated briefs keep converging on the same skeleton with recolored tokens, the skill has learned a template rather than taste. Fix the decision logic that caused the convergence; do not merely add another banned motif.
